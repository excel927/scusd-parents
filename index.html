What's happening

The district is evaluating two programs for a new math adoption.

Option 1

Carnegie Learning

The piloted program. Parents report a workbook (no textbook) paired with software (MATHia) in middle school, and a story-based component with screen time in elementary. Parents cite the vendor's own material describing roughly 40% of time on screens.

Option 2

Amplify Desmos Math

The other finalist. It is built around digital activities and a task-based, discovery-style approach. Parents report it also leans heavily on screens and game-like features, and some students have already moved over from the first program mid-pilot.

The core problem

Both lean on devices

Neither option has been shown to us as a teacher-led, print-first program with a firm limit on screen time. That is the choice we're asking the Board to consider before it votes.

Problems we see in the curriculum

Each concern is labeled by where it comes from: Parent report from families with kids in the pilot, or Research from published studies.

Parent report

Heavy screen time

Parents with children in the pilot describe math time that depends on devices, and cite the vendor's own materials for a figure near 40% in the Carnegie program.

Parent report

No textbook

The pilot uses a consumable workbook only. Families cannot look back at worked examples, explanations, or the sequence of ideas the way a textbook allows.

Parent report

Rushing and distraction

On devices, children hurry to submit answers instead of working through the steps, and devices invite off-task behavior.

Parent report

Gamification

Game-like rewards and activities can make practice feel engaging while hiding whether a child actually understands the math.

Research

Heavy school computer use hasn't raised scores

The OECD's 2015 report Students, Computers and Learning (PISA data) found that countries investing heavily in school technology saw no noticeable gains in reading, math or science, and that students who used computers very frequently at school tended to do worse.

Research

Distraction costs points

PISA 2022 found that students who report being distracted by digital devices in math lessons score meaningfully lower than those who don't.

Research

Novices need direct instruction

Cognitive-load research (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, 2006) and the U.S. National Mathematics Advisory Panel (2008) support explicit teaching, worked examples, and building fluency with core skills such as fractions, rather than leaving children to discover methods on their own.

Research

Results aren't instant or guaranteed

The largest independent study of Carnegie Learning's software-based Algebra I (RAND, Pane et al., 2014) found benefits in the second year of use, not the first. Evidence for the middle-grade and elementary programs is thinner than for that high school course.

Research

Other systems are pulling back

Sweden began shifting funding back to printed textbooks in 2023 after concern about declining reading and learning outcomes and too much screen use in classrooms.

What this does not say: technology has no place in math class. Short, targeted practice can help. Our concern is a curriculum built around screens, adopted without a clear cap, evidence, or print option.

What we're asking the Board to do

Clear, reasonable requests that anyone can make in two minutes.

  1. Slow down the vote. Do not finalize an adoption until families have seen the pilot results.
  2. Publish the pilot data. Student outcomes, teacher feedback and parent feedback, shared with the public before the decision.
  3. Set a written screen-time limit for math, with a print-first standard and a full student textbook (not only a workbook).
  4. Require independent evidence. Use third-party reviews (such as EdReports) and published effectiveness studies as criteria, not vendor claims.
  5. Put parents and math teachers on the adoption committee with a real voice in the recommendation.
  6. Prioritize teacher-led instruction and fluency — worked examples, handwritten practice, and clear explanations of how to break a problem into smaller steps.
  7. Be transparent about total cost, device requirements, and what student data the vendors collect.

How to speak at public comment

You do not need to be an expert. A short, personal story is the most powerful thing the Board can hear.

  1. Sign up using the link above, then arrive by 7:30 PM on Oct 22 to check in and coordinate.
  2. Keep it to about two minutes (confirm the time limit when you sign in). Say your name, your school, and your child's grade.
  3. Tell your story: what you've seen in your child's math homework, workbook or screen time.
  4. Make one clear ask from the list above, and thank the Board for their time.
  5. Can't attend? Call in by phone, or forward this page to someone who can.

A sample 2-minute statement

Good evening. My name is ____, and I have a child in grade __ at ____.

I'm here because I care deeply about how our children learn math. I've looked at what the pilot looks like at home: a workbook with no textbook, a lot of time on a device, and children rushing to submit answers instead of understanding the steps.

Research on schools that invested heavily in classroom technology has not shown gains in math, and distraction from devices is linked to lower scores. Children learn math when a teacher explains it, when they write out their thinking, and when they practice until it's fluent.

I'm asking the Board to publish the pilot results, set a clear limit on screen time, require a printed textbook, and include parents in the decision before any vote. Thank you.
